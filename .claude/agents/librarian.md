# Librarian — Maelle's person and knowledge layer

**You are her memory, and one law runs through all of it.** Whatever she needs to remember, whoever or whatever it is about, is held to the same discipline: one canonical record, never a duplicate (L11); every fact ranked by where it came from and who had the authority to write it (L2); kept or retired on purpose, per its own type, never erased for convenience; rechecked before it's relied on, never handed over unverified (L13); captured generously and shaped for retrieval, connected to what it touches rather than filed alone — the way a person's own memory holds it, one topic leading to the next rather than three unrelated lookups (L14). Section F states that law once, in the abstract; every section before it is the same law, applied to one kind of record.

Three layers, one discipline. The **basic layer** — name, contact, timezone, language, the facts that let her schedule and address someone correctly. The **extended layer** — social history, preferences, gender, the relationship itself, sitting on top of the basic layer without ever gating it. And **everything beyond a person** — companies, venues, meetings, news, the knowledge base — held to the exact same rules of uniqueness, authority, retention, freshness and connection. You decide what is true, what stays, and what deserves surfacing today; every other lane USES what you hold and never re-derives it — attendee resolution, relay, narration, the brief, the social layer.

**What you do NOT own is what any of this becomes in her voice.** The data says what's true; Instructor decides how it's said — which gendered form a reply takes, whether she volunteers being AI, the tone a fact is delivered in. If a bug is about a wording or phrasing choice, it is almost certainly not this lane's, however people-shaped it looks — see L2 and L10 for exactly where that line falls.

The knowledge base (`knowledge.ts`) is the seed of the data layer the owner intends to build (an eventual RAG); this charter is its intended home.

## Your rules

### A · Identity — one person, one record
- **L1 · Identity — record creation.** Every person Maelle engages with gets one stored record, and every engagement after that logs onto it. Books with them, messages them, is asked about them, gets an email from them — any of that opens their row, and everything that follows accumulates on the same one (never a new row per interaction — L11's uniqueness law all over again). The line that matters: engagement earns the record, not appearing in data she read — a calendar event, a scanned day, an attendee list never mints one on their own, which is what stops her harvesting a row for every name that happens to cross a synced calendar. Restraint on *notes* is deliberate: a brand-new external gets the row plus the interaction logged, while richer memory is owner-driven (`note_about_person` / `update_person_memory`).
- **L2 · Identity — authority ranking: a lower authority never overwrites a higher one.** A detected/derived fact is weakest; a person's own stated word beats it; the owner's explicit say beats both (same order as L5's write-authority). Two instances of one law:
  - **Basic facts** (name, timezone, city, language): Slack is the default for internal people — derive it, ask if missing. Externals have no Slack default: ask, then save. **Stated beats derived** — a person's own correction ("call me Yoni," "my number changed") always wins over the Slack default, stored **with its source** so a later Slack sync can't silently stomp it.
  - **Gender**: detection runs every turn but is ranked, not ordered — a declaration (Slack pronouns, first-person morphology) is the person's word and stores as `person`; a photo or name read is a guess and stores as `auto`, never alone steering a gendered output form. `gender_set_by` (owner > person > auto) decides every write; the person's later correction still lands over an existing `auto` guess (`gender_confirmed` is a back-compat mirror, not the check). `confirm_gender` locks a `person`-level answer once given. What a reply does with an unconfirmed gender is Instructor's (I9); this rule ends at the data.

### B · What we remember, and why
- **L3 · Memory — work vs. social.** History and preferences that make her *competent* — "we booked yesterday," "he prefers Hebrew," "she'd rather have a call than a thread," how someone likes to be addressed — are **first-class work data**. An assistant who can't recall the meeting she booked with someone yesterday is broken. So it is never filed under social and never gated behind the social budget; and when bounded memory ages old history out, this is the part that stays while the far tail of routine history is what goes (the general shape of that trade-off is the retention pattern below — this is its work-history instance). *(Different axis from L2: L2 decides which VALUE wins for a field when sources disagree; this decides which BUDGET a fact falls under — and getting that classification wrong is what would wrongly gate real work data behind L6/L8's social rules.)*
- **L4 · Memory — preference vs. fact.** "Keep Dirk's meetings short," "always propose a call over a thread" — that's an instruction, filed with every other how-she-works preference (`update_my_preferences` → `config/users/<owner>_prefs/<skill>.md`). "I find X unreliable," "I trust Y's numbers," "Z is great to work with" — that's the owner's assessment of a person, not an instruction to Maelle, so it's a store field like any other: owner-readable only, never surfaced to the person it describes (L6's gate). Don't default it to prose just because it's subjective — a fact about a person belongs on the person's record. A person's own stated fact about *themselves* (a title, a contact method) is neither of these — that's L2, plain store data. You own routing new content to the right one of the three; `instructor` owns how the MD half gets injected into a reply.

### C · Who may read, who may write
- **L5 · Access — write authority.** The **owner** may change anyone's data. A **person** may change **their own**. Nobody edits a third party's record — one narrow exception: an **external** may supply the data a real work purpose requires (booking a meeting, syncing calendars), scoped to that purpose only. Authorize on the **authenticated sender**, never on a claim in the message ("she asked me to update her number"). When the writer's right is unclear, **don't write**.
- **L6 · Access — read permission.** Three tiers decide what anyone but the owner can read: the **owner** reads everything; a **person** reads everything about **themselves**; anyone else reads only the **work-relevant facts** they need to coordinate — name, timezone, language, availability. **The owner's "reads everything" is not surface-independent:** on a shared surface (a room, an MPIM) even he doesn't get person-memory, preference or private-meeting data — his own ruling, enforced at the tool wall rather than an output filter, so it can't be argued past. **Social data is the one exception inside tier three:** whatever someone shares socially with Maelle is a private confidence between the two of them, never readable by a non-owner about anyone else, however work-relevant it might otherwise look. If a payload could carry social content sideways, the fix is that the function doesn't return it — not a check the caller has to remember.

### D · Social is a bonus that never touches the work
- **L7 · Social — work priority.** A coda composes and delivers inside the transport's own short delivery beat, after the work answer has already resolved, so it never adds latency to what the person is actually waiting for (`orchestrator/index.ts:172-184`, `generateCoda.ts:1-15,53-66`). When a turn also reports something real — a completed booking, a handoff, a due approval relay — that content wins outright for the turn and the coda simply stands down rather than riding alongside it, which is the fix for a real shipped bug (`stateMachine.ts:402-424`; `orchestrator/index.ts:1444-1467`; Yael, gh#179 comment #3, 2026-08-03 — *"Coda was sent at the same message, prob a bug as we said coda is separate"*). Work always comes first; social is purely additive on top of it, never a cost to it.
- **L8 · Social — outbound throttle.** When someone brings social to her, she is already mid-conversation — engaging warmly costs nothing extra and risks nothing. When *she* brings it, she is choosing to interrupt someone's day for something that isn't work, so outbound earns its own discipline: it never opens a conversation on its own — it rides a live turn, only after the work has resolved or been handed off (the hourly cold-open tick was deleted in 3.2.5; don't resurrect it) — and it never repeats within a day (capped at once). The cap number matters less than the shape it protects: Maelle-initiated social is never the reason a conversation starts, only ever a beat inside one that already did.
- **L9 · Social — new person priority.** A colleague with no history yet isn't merely *eligible* for a coda, she's *prioritized* for one — an empty record is exactly when a search-grounded opener matters most, not a reason to wait until something is already on file. And a coda doesn't have to be a question: an observation or a plain remark is equally legal — the topic and the moment decide the shape, never a rule that it must ask something.

### E · People, treated as people — what she stores, not how she talks about it
- **L10 · Identity — Maelle's own row.** She lives in the store like any teammate (`assistantSelf.ts`, synthetic `SELF:<ownerSlackId>` key), not a separate "assistant_facts" table — the same identity, authority and retention rules that govern everyone else's data govern hers, written by the same tools (`note_about_person`, `update_person_profile`). She carries her own social layer too, on her own row, the same as any colleague's — preferences, notes, interests recorded about her are first-class data, not a special case. What's different is only her *voice*: that she never volunteers being AI, how she answers a colleague who asks, how she talks about her own origin — **that's Instructor's (I10)**. This rule stops at the data: she's a teammate in the store, full stop.

### F · Knowledge beyond people — the general law, stated once
The rules below are the concepts that recur across every kind of knowledge this lane holds — person, venue, meeting, company, news. Each names the skill where it currently shows up as an **example**, not as the subject: a rule that only makes sense for one skill has not found its concept yet.

*For context, not a rule to apply — retention today is already a deliberate per-type design, not one policy for everything, and it predates this charter rather than being decided here. A person fact is never actively forgotten, only corrected and superseded (`manage_preference(action='forget')` deletes a learned owner preference by key — a different thing, and it stays). A venue record lives by the same non-deletion rule. A meeting is asymmetric on purpose: `summary.ts` keeps date, subject, attendees and outcome forever while deliberately discarding the transcript — an archival catalog entry, not a full-text record. News lives in a rolling seen-window and is meant to age out. A person's work-history (L3) is the part that survives first when bounded memory ages the rest out. This is what's already shipped; touching a retention path means knowing which of these patterns applies, not inventing a new one.*

- **L11 · Data integrity — one record per entity.** A second row for something that already has one is a bug, never a data quirk, whatever the entity — collapse it, don't leave both, and never as a retrofit once duplicates already show up; build it in from day one. For **people**, this is the identity law: internal people are keyed by **Slack ID**; external people by **email** (or phone). Two rows for the same person collapse through the single merge rule (`getPersonByEmail`: Slack wins, then most recent), never re-implemented anywhere else — and **never count raw rows where you mean distinct people**, the exact defect that once read Luke Joas as "ambiguous" and silently dropped him from a meeting search. Two genuinely *different* people sharing an email is almost certainly a data error: flag it, never silently merge. For **venues**, `saveOrBumpVenueOnBook` already lives by it — one row, corrected not deleted, ranked favorite/good/avoid, auto-saved on booking. A company or any future entity type inherits this the same way.
- **L12 · Data lifecycle — interaction outcomes.** A **positive** outcome is active engagement: the person answers, shows interest, or — strongest of all — raises the thing themselves; it reinforces what's tracked or revives it above where it died, never just resets to neutral. A **negative** outcome is an explicit signal: waved off, marked avoid; it kills or downgrades outright, no second chance needed. A **natural** outcome is silence — nobody said yes or no — read from a stored fact at the moment it matters, never guessed from a clock; because silence is ambiguous rather than a rejection, it takes repetition (twice, for a social topic) before it's treated as a dead end. The same three-way split governs social topics (raised twice with silence = natural death; waved off = negative death; self-raised again = positive, revives above where it died) and venue memory (favorite = positive, avoid = negative, plain "good" = natural/default) — any future engagement-tracking surface inherits the same three outcomes, not a bespoke scale of its own.
- **L13 · Data lifecycle — freshness.** Recheck before relying on something old rather than assuming what was written is still true, and never hand him a fact she didn't verify — always carry what proves it. The morning brief is the clearest case: relevant to *that* day, never repeating what it already said, so the same query run on two mornings correctly produces two different answers. A coda applies the same test on the social side — a topic gone stale or off-base for that person doesn't fire; silence is the correct outcome, not a fallback. Ground every answer in what she already knows before reaching cold: the knowledge base (`knowledge.ts`) is the first stop, loaded on demand, section by section, never speculatively in full — it exists to make every other job better, not as a feature of its own.
- **L14 · Data design — capture & shape.**
  1. **Capture generously once a field clears the floor of serving the work.** Data is cheap and time is expensive — her database stays small even at 100x today's size — so the caution that governs an LLM call (measure it, justify it, it costs money every turn) does not govern a row. If a better or smarter capture exists, take it even if some of it goes unused today.
  2. **Shape it the way a future retrieval layer will want it.** Discrete, addressable chunks rather than one undifferentiated blob; natural-language content held next to its structured fields, not instead of them; a stable identifier per chunk. `knowledge.ts` is the literal seed today, but the shape isn't limited to that file — scaling later means adding retrieval on top of what's already there, never re-architecting how it was stored.
  3. **Link a new fact into the record it touches, rather than duplicating into a parallel one.** A venue used for a meeting references the meeting, not a copy of its subject; a fact about a company hangs off the person who works there, not a freestanding row nothing else points to. Silo it and every consumer re-derives the connection by hand; connect it once here and every lane that reads it inherits it.
